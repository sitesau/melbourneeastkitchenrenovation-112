Scope words: refresh, makeover and full rebuild

Three words decide almost everything downstream. A refresh keeps the existing layout and swaps surface items such as doors, benchtops and splashbacks. A makeover is used in much the same way, with services staying where they are. A full rebuild goes much further: demolition, service rough-in, new cabinetry, benchtops, plumbing, electrical work, splashback, appliances and finishing.

The published FAQ notes that a makeover holding the current layout sits well below a rebuild with custom joinery and structural change, and no standard price list is published. Early figures therefore stay provisional until the room is measured and materials are chosen. One more scope term matters locally: period or heritage work, meaning modern storage and lighting fitted into Edwardian, interwar or Californian bungalow houses without stripping their original character.

Money and paperwork vocabulary

Measure-up: the opening site visit, published as free, where the room is checked against the brief. Itemised fixed-scope quote: a line-by-line price issued after that visit so cost is settled before work begins; read it to separate confirmed selections from assumptions. Payment stages: the scheduled instalments written into the contract. Variations: the formal method for changing scope, price or dates mid-job, because verbal changes are how budgets drift.

Under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995, domestic building work over $10,000 requires a written contract listing the scope, the price, dates, staged payments and how variations are handled. Domestic building insurance is cover required in Victoria for residential building work above a threshold often quoted at $16,000, though the current figure should be checked with the Victorian Building Authority. Warranty and compliance documents are the paperwork due at handover.

Site and trade vocabulary

Rough-in: the stage where plumbing and electrical points are relocated or added before cabinets exist, which is why appliance positions and lighting plans are settled early. Registered trades: Victorian rules require registered practitioners for plumbing and electrical work, and the published service coordinates them under the written contract. Permits: approvals some jobs need; ask which part of the work raises the question and who lodges it.

Joinery: made-to-measure cabinet work such as a butler's pantry or extra drawer banks, where drawings should mark storage, appliance clearances and benchtop joins plainly enough for approval. The published service area runs across Box Hill, Doncaster, Glen Waverley, Blackburn, Ringwood, Croydon, Camberwell, Kew and Balwyn, plus surrounding eastern suburbs of Victoria, so period-sensitive examples are a fair thing to request at quote stage.

Benchtop words after the silica ban

Since 1 July 2024, engineered stone containing 1 per cent or more crystalline silica has been banned across Australia for manufacture, supply, processing and installation under national model work health and safety regulations, with Safe Work Australia as administrator. That one rule rewrote benchtop vocabulary.

Surfaces still on the menu include porcelain, sintered stone, natural granite or marble, solid timber, stainless steel, laminate and low-silica engineered products sitting under the 1 per cent line. Ask how each candidate changes edge profiles, joins, splashback pairing, lead time and upkeep, because cabinetry and stone usually occupy 4 to 6 weeks in fabrication, so a late decision on the surface shifts every date that follows.

Schedule words: lead time and handover

Lead time is the waiting period while cabinetry and stone are fabricated, published at roughly 4 to 6 weeks, when little happens at the house. On-site weeks cover demolition, rough-in, installation and finishing, usually 2 to 4 weeks. End to end, a typical full job lands at about 6 to 12 weeks between signing and completion. Handover is the point where warranty documents and compliance paperwork change hands; confirm at contract stage how they will reach you.
